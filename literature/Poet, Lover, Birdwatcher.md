*- Nissim Ezekiel*
	
	To force the pace and never to be still  
	Is not the way of those who study birds  
	Or women. The best poets wait for words.  
	The hunt is not an exercise of will  
	But patient love relaxing on a hill  
	To note the movement of a timid wing;  
	Until the one who knows that she is loved  
	No longer waits but risks surrendering -  
	In this the poet finds his moral proved  
	Who never spoke before his spirit moved.  
	  
	The slow movement seems, somehow, to say much more.  
	To watch the rarer birds, you have to go  
	Along deserted lanes and where the rivers flow  
	In silence near the source, or by a shore  
	Remote and thorny like the heart's dark floor.  
	And there the women slowly turn around,  
	Not only flesh and bone but myths of light  
	With darkness at the core, and sense is found  
	But poets lost in crooked, restless flight,  
	The deaf can hear, the blind recover sight.

## 1. Introduction and literary background

Nissim Ezekiel (1924–2004) is a major figure in modern Indian English poetry. His poetry is known for its intellectual discipline, psychological insight, precise language, and exploration of human relationships.

“Poet, Lover, Birdwatcher” was published in his poetry collection The Exact Name (1965). It is a reflective, philosophical poem that explores the nature of poetic creation through a comparison between three figures: the poet, the lover, and the birdwatcher.

The poem can be read as an ars poetica — a poem that reflects on the nature and process of poetry itself. However, its significance extends beyond poetry. Ezekiel uses the act of patient observation to explore how human beings approach beauty, love, knowledge, and discovery.

The central argument is that meaningful discovery cannot be forced. It requires patience, attentive observation, emotional sensitivity, and a willingness to endure uncertainty.

## 2. Detailed critical analysis

### A. Patience and the rejection of haste

The poem opens with a rejection of hurried action:

> “To force the pace and never to be still”

This opening establishes the poem's central opposition: haste versus patience. Ezekiel suggests that certain experiences cannot be achieved through force, speed, or restless activity.

The phrase “force the pace” implies an attempt to control the natural rhythm of experience. The poet, lover, and birdwatcher may all desire immediate results, but their pursuits require a different approach.

The statement that the best poets “wait for words” is particularly significant. Poetry is not presented as a mechanical exercise in which words can simply be assembled at will. Rather, the poet must develop the sensitivity to recognize the right word when it becomes available.

**Yet the waiting described here is not laziness or passivity. It is an active discipline of attention. The poet observes, the lover learns, and the birdwatcher remains alert.**

Ezekiel thus transforms patience from an ordinary virtue into a principle of artistic and emotional understanding.

### B. The parallel between the poet, lover, and birdwatcher

The poem's central achievement is its integration of three apparently unrelated activities.

| Figure      | Object of pursuit              | Essential quality     |
| ----------- | ------------------------------ | --------------------- |
| Poet        | Words, meaning, inspiration    | Creative patience     |
| Lover       | Intimacy and understanding     | Emotional sensitivity |
| Birdwatcher | Rare birds and their movements | Careful observation   |

The three figures are united by the fact that their desired objects cannot be obtained simply through force. The birdwatcher cannot command a rare bird to appear. The lover cannot manufacture genuine affection. The poet cannot guarantee inspiration through sheer willpower.

The birdwatcher's activity becomes an extended metaphor for the other two pursuits. The patience required to observe a bird parallels the patience required to understand another person or discover the right poetic expression.

This comparison gives the poem its structural and philosophical unity. Ezekiel does not merely say that patience is useful; he demonstrates how the same principle operates in different areas of human experience.

### C. Birdwatching as a metaphor for poetic creation

The birdwatcher is the poem's central figure of observation. The hunt is not represented as aggressive pursuit but as an exercise in stillness and receptivity.

The birdwatcher must remain quiet, attentive, and sensitive to the slightest movement. This method closely resembles the poet's creative process. The poet must observe ordinary experiences carefully and discover meanings that may initially remain hidden.

The bird's movement is especially important because it is unpredictable. Similarly, inspiration may emerge unexpectedly from an image, a memory, an emotion, or an apparently insignificant experience.

The metaphor also suggests that poetry involves both discipline and uncertainty. A poet may prepare carefully, but the final moment of discovery cannot be entirely controlled.

Thus, Ezekiel presents poetic creation as a combination of patient preparation and sudden illumination.

### D. The symbolism of rare birds and remote places

The poem moves beyond ordinary birdwatching to the search for rare birds in remote and difficult locations.

The rare bird symbolizes something valuable, elusive, and difficult to understand. In the context of poetry, it may represent the perfect word, an original image, or a moment of artistic revelation. In the context of love, it may represent genuine intimacy or a deeper understanding of another person.

The deserted lanes, silent rivers, and remote shores create an atmosphere of solitude and contemplation.

These settings are not merely picturesque natural landscapes. They suggest that profound discoveries often require a departure from habitual surroundings and superficial perception.

The image of the remote shore is particularly significant because it is described as:

> “Remote and thorny like the heart’s dark floor.”

The metaphor of the “heart’s dark floor” introduces psychological depth into the poem. The heart becomes a difficult, partly inaccessible landscape. The adjective “thorny” suggests that emotional and creative discovery may involve pain, confusion, and difficulty.

The poem therefore complicates the idea of patience. Waiting is not always peaceful or comfortable; it may require the individual to confront the difficult and obscure regions of inner experience.

### E. Love, desire, and the mystery of the beloved

Love is not treated as a simple romantic achievement. It is a process of observation, emotional development, and gradual understanding.

The lover, like the birdwatcher, must learn to attend to subtle movements and changes. The beloved cannot be fully understood through impatience or possessiveness. Genuine intimacy requires sensitivity to another person's individuality.

Ezekiel's treatment of love also reveals a tension between understanding and mystery. The beloved may become more familiar and emotionally accessible, yet she does not become completely knowable.

This is an important aspect of the poem's philosophy: love does not necessarily eliminate mystery; it deepens one's awareness of it.

However, a critical reading must also recognize the gendered perspective of the poem. The woman is described as an object of study and pursuit, much like the bird being watched. This comparison may reduce the beloved's independent agency, especially when love is framed as a process through which the woman eventually responds to the lover.

The metaphor is therefore both illuminating and limited. It effectively conveys the need for patience, but it also raises questions about whether love should be understood as observation and pursuit or as a reciprocal relationship between two independent individuals.

### F. The poet's inner struggle and the search for meaning

The poem's later movement turns increasingly toward the poet's internal experience. The poet's search is not a smooth or predictable journey.

Ezekiel describes the poet's difficult movement through uncertainty and confusion. The phrase:

> “By poets lost in crooked, restless flight”

is particularly revealing.

The word “lost” suggests that the poet may not always know where the creative process is leading. “Crooked” rejects the idea that artistic discovery follows a perfectly straight path, while “restless flight” conveys movement, experimentation, and an inability to remain entirely settled.

This complicates the poem's earlier emphasis on stillness. The birdwatcher must remain physically still, but the poet's mind may be restless and uncertain.

The apparent contradiction is significant. Ezekiel is not advocating absolute inactivity. Rather, he distinguishes between impatient external action and the complex internal movement required for artistic discovery.

The poet may appear to be lost, but this wandering can eventually lead to a deeper understanding of experience.

### G. The transformative power of poetic vision

The poem culminates in an extraordinary transformation of perception:

> “the deaf can hear, the blind recover sight”

These images suggest that poetry can restore or intensify the capacity to perceive.

The deaf and blind may be understood literally as figures of sensory deprivation, but within the poem's philosophical framework, they also represent people who are unable to recognize deeper meanings in ordinary life.

Poetic illumination makes the previously unnoticed visible and the previously unheard audible. It does not simply produce beautiful language; it changes the individual's relationship with reality.

The ending therefore gives poetry a transformative function. The poet's patient search ultimately becomes a form of discovery that can renew perception and understanding.

The idea also connects the three figures. The birdwatcher learns to see what others overlook; the lover discovers emotional dimensions that may initially remain hidden; the poet gives language to experiences that were previously difficult to articulate.

## 3. Critical analysis of the poem's structure and style
### A. Form and structure

The poem consists of twenty lines arranged in two ten-line stanzas. Its compact structure gives it the character of a carefully constructed philosophical argument. It is frequently discussed in terms of its sonnet-like qualities and disciplined metrical movement.

The first stanza establishes the principle of patience and develops the analogy between the three figures. The second stanza expands the metaphor through images of remote landscapes, the heart's interior, and the poet's experience of uncertainty and illumination.

The movement from external observation to inner revelation gives the poem a logical and psychological progression.

### B. Enjambment and rhythmic movement

Ezekiel frequently allows sentences to flow across line endings. This technique, known as enjambment, creates a sense of continuity and prevents each line from functioning as an isolated statement.

The flowing syntax is appropriate to the poem's subject because observation, love, and poetic inspiration are presented as processes that unfold gradually.

The reader is encouraged to move from one line to the next, much as the poet or birdwatcher follows a developing perception.

The formal discipline of the poem thus works alongside its flowing syntax, balancing control with movement.

### C. Imagery and sensory language

Ezekiel's imagery is precise but evocative. The poem's landscape includes birds, deserted lanes, rivers, shores, and the darkness of the heart.

These images operate on two levels:

- Literal level: They create a natural setting for the birdwatcher's patient observation.
    
- Symbolic level: They represent the difficult journey toward poetic insight and emotional understanding.
    

The contrast between silence and perception is especially effective. Silence becomes a condition in which the observer can notice what ordinary noise conceals.

The images of hearing and sight at the conclusion extend this sensory framework, suggesting that the process of discovery can transform perception itself.

### D. Diction and tone

The diction is controlled, reflective, and philosophical. Ezekiel avoids excessive ornamentation, allowing the images and the development of the argument to carry the poem's meaning.

Words associated with patience, silence, darkness, remoteness, and restlessness create a contemplative atmosphere. The tone is serious without becoming excessively sentimental.

The poem's intellectual quality is one of its defining features. Emotion is present, particularly in its treatment of love and poetic inspiration, but it is examined through reflection rather than emotional exaggeration.

## 4. Major themes

Theme 1

Patience as a condition of discovery

The poem argues that meaningful results in art, love, and observation require time. Patience is a disciplined readiness to recognize what cannot be forced.

Theme 2

Artistic inspiration and creativity

Poetry emerges through attentive engagement with experience. The poet's task is not simply to produce words but to discover the words that express a deeper truth.

Theme 3

Love and the mystery of human relationships

Love requires emotional sensitivity and patience. Yet the beloved remains partly unknowable, suggesting that intimacy and mystery can coexist.

Theme 4

Observation and perception

The birdwatcher symbolizes the ability to notice what escapes ordinary attention. The poet similarly discovers meaning through close observation.

Theme 5

Solitude, uncertainty, and self-discovery

Remote landscapes and the dark heart symbolize the difficult inner journey involved in creativity and emotional understanding. Discovery may require wandering through uncertainty.

## 5. Deeper critical interpretation

### A. The poem as a statement of artistic philosophy

At its deepest level, “Poet, Lover, Birdwatcher” is a meditation on how art comes into existence. Ezekiel rejects the simplistic notion that poetry is produced entirely through conscious effort.

Instead, poetic creation involves receptivity: the ability to remain open to experience until a meaningful pattern or expression becomes apparent.

This does not mean that craftsmanship is unimportant. The poem itself is formally disciplined. Rather, Ezekiel implies that technique alone cannot guarantee genuine poetic insight. The poet needs both artistic preparation and the sensitivity to recognize an illuminating moment.

### B. The paradox of stillness and movement

One of the most interesting tensions in the poem is the relationship between stillness and movement.

The birdwatcher must be still, but the bird moves. The poet waits, yet the mind may wander through restless and crooked paths. The lover exercises patience, but the emotional relationship continues to develop.

Stillness, therefore, is not the absence of movement. It is a state of attention that allows meaningful movement to be perceived.

This paradox makes the poem more complex than a straightforward moral lesson about patience. It suggests that genuine discovery depends on a balance between disciplined waiting and openness to unpredictable experience.

### C. The limits of the central metaphor

The birdwatching metaphor is effective because it unifies the poem's three subjects. Nevertheless, it has limitations.

A birdwatcher observes a creature that may remain indifferent to the observer. A lover, however, participates in a relationship involving two conscious individuals. Similarly, the poet's relationship with language is not exactly equivalent to either birdwatching or romantic pursuit.

The poem's analogy should therefore not be taken as a literal equivalence. Its value lies in the shared qualities of patience, sensitivity, and attentiveness, rather than in the assumption that poetry, love, and birdwatching are identical activities.

The distinction is particularly important when considering the beloved's agency. Love is most fully understood not as the successful acquisition of another person but as a reciprocal encounter in which both individuals remain free.

This tension does not invalidate the poem's central insight. Instead, it opens up a more nuanced discussion of the relationship between observation, desire, and understanding.

## 6. Significance of the title

The title “Poet, Lover, Birdwatcher” is simple, but its arrangement is meaningful. The three nouns place artistic creation, emotional experience, and the observation of nature within a single conceptual framework.

The poet appears first, reflecting the poem's concern with artistic creation. The lover introduces the emotional dimension, while the birdwatcher provides the concrete activity through which the other two can be understood.

The title does not identify one figure as superior to the others. Instead, it emphasizes their shared disposition as patient seekers.

Its significance lies in the way it brings together three distinct pursuits and reveals a common philosophy of attention and discovery.

## 7. Conclusion: Overall critical appreciation

Nissim Ezekiel's “Poet, Lover, Birdwatcher” is a compact but philosophically rich poem that explores the relationship between patience, perception, love, and artistic creation. Through the sustained comparison of three different figures, Ezekiel develops a coherent vision of discovery as a process that requires discipline, sensitivity, and openness to uncertainty.

The poem's strength lies in its ability to move from the concrete image of birdwatching to the abstract experience of poetic inspiration and emotional understanding. Its imagery, controlled diction, reflective tone, and formal organization reinforce its central argument.

At the same time, its treatment of love invites critical reflection on the difference between observing another person and participating in a reciprocal relationship.

Ultimately, the poem suggests that the most meaningful experiences in life cannot be produced through impatience or force. They emerge when individuals learn to observe carefully, wait attentively, and remain receptive to what is not immediately visible.

In essence, Ezekiel presents poetry, love, and birdwatching as three forms of patient seeking, through which human beings discover beauty and meaning that would otherwise remain hidden.
