	Shall I compare thee to a summer’s day? 
	Thou art more lovely and more temperate. 
	Rough winds do shake the darling buds of May, 
	And summer’s lease hath all too short a date; 
	
	Sometime too hot the eye of heaven shines, 
	And often is his gold complexion dimmed;
	And every fair from fair sometime declines, 
	By chance or nature’s changing course untrimmed; 
	
	But thy eternal summer shall not fade, 
	Nor lose possession of that fair thou ow’st, 
	Nor shall Death brag thou wand’rest in his shade, 
	When in eternal lines to time thou grow’st. 
	
	So long as men can breathe or eyes can see, 
	So long lives this, and this gives life to thee.


## 1. Summary of the Poem

The speaker begins by asking whether he should compare the beloved to a summer's day. He quickly decides that the beloved is more beautiful and more balanced than summer itself.

Summer, despite its beauty, is imperfect and temporary. Winds damage flowers, the season is brief, the sun can become unbearably hot, and its brightness can be obscured. Shakespeare then generalizes this observation: all beautiful things eventually lose their beauty, whether through accident or the natural passage of time.

The poem takes a decisive turn in the third quatrain. The speaker declares that the beloved's "eternal summer" will never fade. Unlike ordinary beauty, which is subject to time and death, the beloved will survive through the poem itself.

In the final couplet, Shakespeare makes his most powerful assertion: as long as human beings live and read, the poem will continue to exist, preserving the beloved's beauty for future generations.

In essence, Sonnet 18 transforms a love poem into a declaration of poetic immortality.

	Lines 1–2 · The opening comparison

"Shall I compare thee to a summer's day? / Thou art more lovely and more temperate."

The poem opens with a rhetorical question. The speaker considers the conventional poetic act of comparing a beloved to a beautiful natural object. Summer is traditionally associated with warmth, vitality, growth, and beauty.

However, the speaker immediately claims that the beloved surpasses summer. The word "more" establishes the beloved's superiority, while "temperate" suggests moderation, balance, and gentleness.

"Temperate" is especially significant because it introduces a contrast between the beloved's stability and the unpredictable extremes of nature. The beloved is not merely physically attractive but appears harmonious and composed.

Critical insight: Shakespeare begins with a familiar love-poetry convention but revises it. Nature is no longer the perfect standard against which human beauty is measured; the beloved becomes the superior standard.

	Lines 3–4 · Nature's instability

"Rough winds do shake the darling buds of May, / And summer's lease hath all too short a date."

The idealized image of summer is interrupted by destructive winds. The "darling buds" represent delicacy, youth, and beauty. The adjective "darling" personifies the buds as precious and vulnerable, making their exposure to rough winds seem particularly cruel.

The metaphor of summer's "lease" is especially important. A lease is a temporary legal arrangement that grants possession for a limited period. Shakespeare thus presents summer's beauty as something that exists only for a fixed duration.

The word "date" reinforces the idea of an unavoidable deadline. Summer does not possess its beauty permanently; its time will expire.

Critical insight: The legal and economic metaphor of the lease introduces the poem's central conflict: beauty may be possessed, but it cannot ordinarily be kept forever.

	Lines 5–6 · The sun's imperfections

"Sometime too hot the eye of heaven shines, / And often is his gold complexion dimmed;"

The "eye of heaven" refers to the sun. The metaphor personifies the sun as a living being with an eye and a face. Its "gold complexion" evokes radiance, warmth, and splendour.

Yet the sun is not consistently beautiful. Sometimes it is excessively hot; at other times, clouds or atmospheric conditions dim its brightness.

The repetition of "Sometime" and "often" emphasizes the irregularity of nature. Beauty is vulnerable not only to destruction but also to inconsistency and excess.

Critical insight: Shakespeare avoids presenting summer as an uncomplicated ideal. Even the sun, a traditional symbol of perfection and glory, cannot sustain an unchanging state of beauty.

	Lines 7–8 · The universal law of decay

"And every fair from fair sometime declines, / By chance or nature's changing course untrimmed;"

These lines move from particular examples of summer's flaws to a universal observation about beauty.

"Every fair" means every beautiful thing. The repetition of "fair" draws attention to the process by which beauty loses its original condition.

Shakespeare identifies two sources of decline: "chance" and "nature's changing course." Beauty may be damaged by unpredictable events or gradually altered by the natural processes of time and change.

The word "untrimmed" suggests something stripped of its adornment, order, or former attractiveness. It also reinforces the idea that beauty cannot always be protected against change.

Critical insight: The poem shifts from the imperfections of summer to the vulnerability of all earthly beauty. This prepares the reader for the extraordinary claim that the beloved will escape the fate shared by everything else.

	Lines 9–10 · The volta and poetic preservation

"But thy eternal summer shall not fade / Nor lose possession of that fair thou ow'st,"

The word "But" marks the volta, or major rhetorical turn, of the sonnet. After eight lines describing impermanence, the speaker introduces an apparently opposing reality.

"Eternal summer" is a metaphor for the beloved's enduring beauty. It is not necessarily a claim that the beloved's physical body will never age. Rather, the poem promises to preserve the beloved's beauty beyond the ordinary limits of life.

The phrase "lose possession" echoes the earlier "lease." Summer's beauty was temporary, but the beloved's beauty is now represented as a possession that cannot be taken away.

The verb "ow'st" means "own" or "possess." It suggests that the beloved possesses a beauty that poetry can preserve.

Critical insight: The volta transforms the poem from a meditation on the failure of natural beauty into a bold assertion of art's capacity to resist time.

	Lines 11–12 · Death and eternity

"Nor shall Death brag thou wand'rest in his shade, / When in eternal lines to time thou grow'st."

Death is personified as a proud conqueror who might boast of having claimed the beloved. The capitalized "Death" gives it a powerful, almost mythological presence.

The word "brag" is strikingly colloquial and competitive. Shakespeare imagines Death as an arrogant opponent whose victory can be challenged.

"His shade" evokes death as a dark realm or shadowy domain. The beloved, however, will not be wholly confined to that domain because the poem preserves their presence.

The phrase "eternal lines" has a double significance: it refers to the lines of the sonnet and to their enduring quality. "To time thou grow'st" suggests that the beloved's existence expands into the future through poetry.

Critical insight: The poet's confidence is almost defiant. Death may destroy the body, but it cannot fully erase the beloved's poetic representation.

	Lines 13–14 · The closing couplet

"So long as men can breathe or eyes can see, / So long lives this, and this gives life to thee."

The final couplet delivers the poem's ultimate claim. The phrase "men can breathe" represents human life, while "eyes can see" suggests the act of reading and witnessing.

The demonstrative "this" refers primarily to the poem itself. Its survival depends on the continuation of human readers, making literary immortality dependent on both the text and its audience.

The repetition of "So long" creates a powerful sense of duration. The balanced structure of the couplet reinforces the apparent certainty of the speaker's promise.

The final phrase, "gives life to thee," completes the transformation of poetry into a life-preserving force.

Critical insight: The ending is both a declaration of love and a declaration of artistic ambition. Shakespeare claims that the poem can preserve the beloved, while the poem's continued existence also preserves the poet's own creative achievement.
