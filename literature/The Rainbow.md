*- William Wordsworth*

	My heart leaps up when I behold 
	A rainbow in the sky: 
	So was it when my life began; 
	So is it now I am a man; 
	So be it when I shall grow old, 
	Or let me die!
	The Child is father of the Man; 
	And I could wish my days to be 
	Bound each to each by natural piety.

## 1. Summary and Explanation of the Poem

The poem describes the speaker’s deep emotional response to the sight of a rainbow. Whenever he sees one, his heart fills with spontaneous joy. This feeling is not limited to his adulthood; he remembers experiencing the same happiness in childhood.

The speaker then reflects on the continuity of this emotion. He feels joy as a child, continues to feel it as an adult, and hopes that it will remain with him in old age. His declaration, “Or let me die!” emphasizes how essential this emotional connection with nature is to his identity.

The famous statement, “The Child is father of the Man,” expresses the idea that childhood shapes adulthood. The child is not merely an earlier stage of life but the foundation of the adult personality. The poet concludes by wishing that every stage of his life should remain connected through “natural piety”—a deep, reverential love for nature.

Thus, the poem moves from a simple observation of nature to a profound reflection on human life, memory, and spiritual development.

## 2. Detailed Critical Analysis: Stanza-by-Stanza and Line-by-Line

The poem is a single stanza of nine lines, but its thought can be divided into three meaningful movements.

### A. Lines 1–2: The Joy of Nature

> My heart leaps up when I behold A rainbow in the sky:

The opening line immediately establishes the speaker’s emotional relationship with nature. The phrase “My heart leaps up” conveys a sudden, spontaneous, and intense feeling of joy.

The poet does not merely say that he likes the rainbow. Instead, he describes a physical and emotional response that seems to arise instinctively. The heart, traditionally regarded as the seat of emotion, becomes the centre of this experience.

The verb “leaps” is especially significant. It suggests energy, excitement, vitality, and an almost childlike enthusiasm. The speaker’s response is neither intellectual nor calculated; it is immediate and heartfelt.

The word “behold” also deserves attention. It suggests more than casual looking. It conveys an act of attentive observation, as though the speaker is fully absorbed in the natural phenomenon before him.

The rainbow itself is an important symbol. It represents beauty, wonder, hope, and the enduring power of nature to awaken human emotions. Although a rainbow is a familiar natural occurrence, Wordsworth presents it as a source of extraordinary spiritual joy.

The simplicity of the image is characteristic of Wordsworth’s Romanticism. He finds profound meaning not in grand historical events or elaborate artificial settings, but in an ordinary experience of the natural world.

The colon at the end of the second line introduces the reflection that follows. The sight of the rainbow becomes the starting point for the speaker’s meditation on the entire course of his life.

### B. Lines 3–6: Continuity of Emotion Throughout Life

> So was it when my life began; So is it now I am a man; So be it when I shall grow old, Or let me die!

These lines form the emotional and philosophical centre of the poem.

The repeated word “So” creates a strong sense of continuity. It connects the speaker’s past, present, and future, showing that his response to nature has remained constant.

#### “So was it when my life began”

The speaker looks back to his childhood and recalls that the sight of a rainbow filled him with joy even then. Childhood is presented as a period of instinctive wonder and emotional openness.

Wordsworth often portrays childhood as a particularly significant stage of human development. In his poetry, children frequently possess a freshness of perception and a closeness to nature that adults may gradually lose.

#### “So is it now I am a man”

The movement from the past to the present demonstrates that the speaker has preserved his childlike sensitivity. Although he has matured physically and intellectually, he has not become emotionally indifferent to nature.

This is important because Wordsworth does not simply celebrate childhood as a lost paradise. He celebrates the possibility of carrying childhood's wonder into adult life.

The contrast between “began” and “now” also emphasizes the passage of time. The speaker’s life has changed, but his fundamental emotional response remains the same.

#### “So be it when I shall grow old”

The speaker now turns toward the future. The phrase “So be it” is both a wish and a declaration. He hopes that his love of nature will remain unchanged even in old age.

The future tense introduces a note of uncertainty. Although he has preserved his feelings so far, he cannot be entirely certain that he will always do so. His wish therefore becomes an expression of his deepest personal values.

#### “Or let me die!”

This is the most dramatic line in the poem. The short, forceful exclamation breaks the rhythm of the preceding lines and reveals the intensity of the speaker’s conviction.

The statement is best understood as a passionate poetic declaration rather than a literal discussion of death. The speaker feels that a life stripped of its capacity for wonder, joy, and reverence would lose something essential to its meaning.

The line also introduces a subtle tension into the poem. The speaker celebrates continuity, yet his emphatic wish reveals a fear that this continuity might be lost. His joy is therefore accompanied by an awareness of human change and mortality.

### C. Line 7: The Central Paradox

> The Child is father of the Man;

This is the poem’s most famous line and its central philosophical statement.

At first glance, the line appears illogical. A child cannot literally be the father of an adult. Wordsworth deliberately reverses the expected relationship between childhood and adulthood to express a deeper truth.

The paradox suggests that the adult personality develops from the experiences, emotions, perceptions, and values formed in childhood. In this sense, the child is the creator of the future adult.

The line also challenges the conventional idea that maturity necessarily means superiority. Wordsworth implies that adults may learn from the child’s capacity for wonder, sincerity, and emotional openness.

The capitalized words “Child” and “Man” give the statement a universal significance. The poet moves beyond his personal experience and makes a claim about human development in general.

The line is not simply an observation about age. It expresses a philosophy of identity: the past remains active within the present, and the adult self is inseparable from the child it once was.

The paradox also suggests that childhood is not merely a stage to be left behind. It is a continuing source of emotional and spiritual life.

### D. Lines 8–9: Natural Piety and the Unity of Life

> And I could wish my days to be Bound each to each by natural piety.

The final two lines provide the conclusion of the poem and explain the speaker’s deepest desire.

#### “And I could wish my days to be”

The phrase “I could wish” expresses a personal aspiration rather than a command or universal rule. Wordsworth does not claim that everyone must experience nature in precisely the same way. He presents his own desire for a life shaped by emotional continuity.

The word “days” is significant because it refers to the individual units of a lifetime. The speaker wishes to connect not only childhood, adulthood, and old age in broad terms, but also the individual experiences that make up his existence.

#### “Bound each to each by natural piety”

This final line contains the philosophical conclusion of the poem.

The word “bound” suggests unity, connection, and an unbroken relationship. The speaker wants every stage of his life to be linked by a common feeling of reverence for nature.

The phrase “natural piety” is especially important. Here, “piety” does not refer exclusively to formal religious worship. It suggests devotion, reverence, gratitude, and a sense of sacred attachment. The word “natural” connects this devotion to the natural world and to the spontaneous emotions it awakens.

Wordsworth therefore presents nature as a source of spiritual nourishment. His relationship with nature is not merely aesthetic; it has moral and spiritual dimensions.

The poem ends not with a description of the rainbow itself, but with an expression of the speaker’s wish for a meaningful and integrated life. Nature becomes the force that connects memory, present experience, and future hope.
